01Empathy & Definition

The broken experience

Students and commuters who need to travel the same route at the same time — from campus or a hostel to a market, station, or nearby locality — have no reliable way to find each other in the moment. They either travel alone paying full fare, wait around hoping to spot someone, or rely on ad-hoc WhatsApp groups that are slow, noisy, and easily ignored.

Proto-persona assumption, before research

Aisha, 20 — second-year student living in hostel

Goal: Get to a common off-campus destination cheaply and safely, especially in the evening.

Assumed behavior: Posts in a WhatsApp group asking if anyone is headed the same way, then waits.

Assumed frustration: The message gets buried and no one replies in time, so she ends up booking solo or missing her window.

Interviews

Three structured interviews, plus supplementary conversations with additional commuters, surfaced one dominant, recurring failure pattern:

I just text in the WhatsApp group, but people aren't very active there — I usually end up going alone. I'm even ready to share a cab with someone outside campus.

— Aaryan Rai, weekly commuter to Pune

I'm not free enough to keep updating my status on a webpage if I'm going or not.

— additional interviewee

Every other person spoken to described the same fallback behavior: post once in a group chat, receive no timely response, then default to calling or texting individual friends directly — or simply traveling alone. There is no real system in place, only informal, one-off outreach through a single channel that is frequently too quiet to work.

User persona validated by real interviews

Rohan, 21

Frequent commuter — travels the same off-campus route nearly every weekend

Current behavior
Posts once in a WhatsApp group and waits. The group is inactive, so he usually ends up going alone.
Key structural trait
Willing to share a ride with someone outside his friend circle entirely — the problem is not "who's my friend," it's visibility beyond one small, quiet group.
Goal
Reliably find a co-traveler before he has to leave, without depending on a single inactive group chat as his only channel.

Core pain point

Frequent commuters have no reliable way to find a committed co-traveler in time, because their only channel — one relatively inactive WhatsApp group — consistently fails to produce a timely response, forcing them to travel alone.

User stories

  1. As a frequent commuter, I want my route and travel time visible beyond my one friend group, so that I'm not dependent on a single inactive chat.
  2. As someone looking for a travel partner, I want to see other commuters heading the same way in real time, so that I have options beyond whoever happens to be active in one group.
  3. As a traveler open to going with people outside my immediate circle, I want to be notified the moment a match appears, so that I don't have to keep checking manually.
  4. As a commuter, I want the system to surface matches automatically rather than requiring me to maintain an active profile, so that it stays useful without depending on my own discipline.
02Ideate & Evaluate

Feature & content list

Fifteen items were drawn directly from the four user stories above, then used as the raw material for a hybrid card sort.

  1. Post a trip
  2. Browse trips
  3. Confirm / commit
  4. Cancel a trip
  5. Match notification
  6. Chat with match
  7. Live location share
  8. Fare split calculator
  9. Rate co-traveler
  10. Report no-show
  11. Trust badge (college / hostel)
  12. Emergency check-in
  13. Save frequent route
  14. Visibility setting (friends / everyone)
  15. Departure reminder

Card sort — method

A hybrid card sort was run with eleven participants via a short Google Form. Four proposed categories — Trip Setup, Communication, Trust & Safety, Reminders & Tracking — plus an open Other option were offered for all fifteen items, alongside free-text questions on naming and fit.

Patterns found

Strong, near-unanimous groupings

Post a trip, browse trips, cancel a trip, and save a frequent route were placed in Trip Setup by the clear majority. Chat with match landed firmly in Communication. Trust badge, rate co-traveler, and emergency check-in landed firmly in Trust & Safety.

Contested items

Live location share split almost exactly between Trust & Safety and Reminders & Tracking — one respondent noted directly that "safety and reminder seemed like they were in two groups at once." Confirm/commit spread across three categories, suggesting it reads as both a setup action and a trust signal.

Weak-fit items

Report no-show scattered across four different categories with no clear winner — the strongest early signal that this item's placement would need testing, not assuming.

What it reveals

Users organize this space primarily around when in the trip lifecycle something happens, rather than around who performs the action. Confusion clusters specifically wherever an item blurs safety and status-tracking — because "is this person reliable" and "is this trip on track" aren't separate concepts in users' heads.

Tree test — method & tasks

Five new participants (not reused from the card sort, to avoid bias) were shown the top-level categories only, given a task, and asked which category they'd check first — sub-items were revealed only after each first choice.

  1. Task 1: Someone you matched with never showed up for the trip. Where would you go to report this? Expected: Trust & Safety → Report no-show
  2. Task 2: You want a friend to see your live location during the ride, for safety. Where would you turn this on? Expected: Trust & Safety → Live location share
  3. Task 3: You want to be notified the moment someone matches your route. Where would you check this? Expected: Reminders & Tracking → Match notification

Where it failed — and the IA pivot

Not one participant expected Report no-show to live under Trust & Safety. Three of five reached for Communication instead — in the moment, a no-show reads as a messaging problem ("I'd message them about it"), not a formal safety report. The other two reached for Reminders & Tracking, treating it as a trip-status issue.

Pivot: Report no-show was moved out of Trust & Safety and into Communication, matching the majority's actual mental model rather than the logically "correct" but untested placement.

03Prioritization

MoSCoW prioritization

Must-have max 4

  • Post a trip
  • Browse trips
  • Confirm / commit
  • Match notification

Should-have

  • Chat with match
  • Cancel a trip
  • Report no-show
  • Trust badge

Could-have

  • Fare split calculator
  • Rate co-traveler
  • Save frequent route
  • Departure reminder
  • Visibility setting

Won't-have this version

  • Live location share
  • Emergency check-in

Live location share and emergency check-in were cut despite scoring well in research: both are real-time, liability-heavy safety features requiring infrastructure far beyond an MVP's scope. The validated pain point was about finding and trusting a co-traveler — not monitoring a trip already in progress. That is a real, later problem worth solving, just not this one.

The hardest call — DFV matrix

Match notification and Trust badge were debated hardest for the final Must-Have slot. Match notification answers the core pain point directly — the failure of a single inactive channel to produce a timely response. Trust badge, while valuable, addresses a related but secondary concern rather than the validated root cause.

FeatureDesirabilityFeasibilityViabilityTotal
Match notification 53513
Trust badge 44311

Trust badge is easier to build, but Match notification wins on the two dimensions that matter most for an MVP: it directly answers the single most-repeated frustration across every interview, and it is what would bring people back to the product instead of falling back to WhatsApp. Trust badge moves to Should-have.

Final Must-Have set

  1. Post a trip
  2. Browse trips
  3. Confirm / commit
  4. Match notification
